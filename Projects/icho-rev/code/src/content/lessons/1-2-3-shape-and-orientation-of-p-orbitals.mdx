# Shape and orientation of p-orbitals
## 1. Prerequisites

1.2.2 — orbitals as wavefunctions, radial nodes, and the habit of asking "what does the curve look
like" rather than "where does the electron go".

## 2. Hook

Hold a pen along the x-axis, one along y, one along z. That is a p subshell: three orbitals of
identical shape, each pointing along a different axis, each with a plane through the nucleus where
its wavefunction is zero. Rotate the atom 90° and you relabel which is which, which is why the three
are degenerate in an isolated atom and split in a field. Where this analogy breaks: the pens are
solid objects occupying space, while the lobes are regions of high probability amplitude and the
"plane" is a surface of exactly zero amplitude, not a wall.

## 3. Core idea

A p orbital has l = 1, so one angular node — a plane through the nucleus — and n − l − 1 radial
nodes. It is two-lobed, with opposite signs of ψ in the two lobes, and comes in three orientations
that are degenerate in the isolated central field. The density is zero at the nucleus, which is why
p orbitals penetrate a core much less than s orbitals and why their energy order differs in
multi-electron atoms.

## 4. Definition

A **p orbital** is a one-electron state with l = 1; its density is zero at the nucleus, unlike an s
orbital's. The **angular node** is the nodal plane
containing the nucleus; it contributes l = 1 to the total node count. **Phase** is the sign of ψ in
each lobe: the two lobes have opposite phase. A **degenerate** set means equal energy, true for the
three p orbitals only in the isolated central field.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: the shape follows from l = 1.** The angular part of the wavefunction is proportional to
sin θ times a function of φ, which vanishes on a plane through the nucleus and is largest
perpendicular to it. Squaring gives two lobes; the sign of the unsquared function is opposite in the
two lobes. Each lobe is not a separate electron and not a separate orbital.

**Step 2: three orientations from m_l.** The quantum number m_l takes −1, 0, +1. Those three
solutions are complex (they carry e^(±iφ)), which is awkward for chemistry, so we take real linear
combinations: p_z = the m_l = 0 function, and p_x and p_y from combinations of m_l = ±1. The
combinations are still exact solutions of the same equation with the same energy; only their
orientation differs. A consequence worth knowing: p_x and p_y are not eigenfunctions of the
z-component of angular momentum, because they are mixtures of m_l = ±1. They are still eigenfunctions
of L² with eigenvalue l(l + 1)ħ² = 2ħ², so they are legitimate orbitals, not defective ones; the
real basis is chosen because it points along bonds. That is a level-3 detail
usually met in spectroscopy, not a contradiction.

**Step 3: node bookkeeping.** 2p: n = 2, l = 1, so radial nodes = 0 and angular = 1; total 1 = n − 1.
3p: radial = 1, angular = 1; total 2. The 3p radial node is a sphere at r = 6a₀/Z, which for
hydrogen is 317 pm. The radial function changes sign there, so the inner and outer lobes have
opposite phase; that phase reversal, not a literal second dumbbell, is why 3p looks like a small
dumbbell sitting inside a larger one.

**Step 4: why "zero at the nucleus" matters.** Every p orbital has ψ(0) = 0, so an electron in a p
orbital is never found at the nucleus, while an s electron is. In a multi-electron atom the s
electron therefore feels the full nuclear charge part of the time and the p electron does not; this
penetration difference is what pushes E(2s) below E(2p) in lithium and beyond. It is one factor
behind the s/p energy split; across-period size and electronegativity trends also depend on the
rising effective nuclear charge, so do not read them from penetration alone.

**Step 5: the subshell, not the orbital, holds six electrons.** Three orbitals times two spins is
6. Confusing orbitals with subshells produces answers that are wrong by a factor of three, and it is
the most common slip in electron-configuration questions.

The three p orbitals share one origin and three mutually perpendicular axes. Each dumbbell has

p_z nodal plane. The orientation is a label, not a change of shape. The drawing is schematic: the
lobes are regions of probability amplitude, not solid objects.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The three orbitals are degenerate only in an isolated atom. A ligand field, a crystal field or a
magnetic field splits them, and the split is what crystal field theory quantifies. The real
functions are a choice of basis, not the only possible one. And a p orbital is not a "dumbbell
shaped cloud of charge": it is a probability amplitude whose square gives density, and a single
measurement finds one electron at one point.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** Hydrogen 3p, with the radial function R_3p ∝ σ(6 − σ)e^(−σ/3), σ = Zr/a₀, a₀ = 52.9 pm.

**Hanapin.** The radial node radius, the total node count, and the number of electrons a 3p subshell
can hold.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: identify n and l first — n = 3, l = 1 — then use the node formulas rather
than solving anything.

Radial nodes = n − l − 1 = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1. The radial function vanishes where σ(6 − σ) = 0, so σ = 0
(at the nucleus, not counted as a radial node) or σ = 6. Hence r = 6a₀/Z = 6 × 52.9 = 317.4 pm for
hydrogen.

Angular nodes = l = 1, the nodal plane. Total = 1 + 1 = 2 = n − 1.

Electrons in 3p: 2(2l + 1) = 2(3) = 6.

**Sagot.** One radial node at 317 pm, one angular node, two nodes in total, and six electrons per
3p subshell.

## 9. Traps

1. "A p orbital holds 6 electrons." A p subshell holds 6; one orbital holds 2.
2. Drawing the lobes joined at the nucleus. ψ = 0 at the nucleus, so there is a gap, not a waist.
3. Counting the node at r = 0 as a radial node. The σ = 0 root of the radial function is the
   nucleus, not a node.
4. Forgetting that p orbitals have no density at the nucleus, then explaining penetration with s and
   p on equal footing.
5. Calling p_x and p_y eigenfunctions of L_z. They are real combinations of the m_l = ±1
   eigenfunctions.
6. Assuming the three p orbitals split in every atom. In an isolated atom they are degenerate; a
   field is needed to split them.

## 10. Check questions

1. Give the radial and angular node counts for 2p, 3p and 4p.
   2p: 0 radial, 1 angular. 3p: 1 radial, 1 angular. 4p: 2 radial, 1 angular. The angular count
   never changes for p; the radial count rises with n.
2. How many electrons can the n = 3, l = 1 level hold, and why is that not the same as "the 3p
   orbital"?
   Six, from 2(2l + 1); the subshell contains three orbitals, each holding two.
3. Why does the 3p radial node sit at 317 pm while the 2p has none?
   n − l − 1 = 1 for 3p and 0 for 2p; the extra radial node is the spherical surface where the
   3p radial function changes sign.
4. A p orbital points at a neighbouring atom. Is the resulting bond σ or π?
   σ: the lobes point along the internuclear axis and overlap head-on. A p orbital perpendicular to
   that axis would give π overlap.

## 11. Connections

1.2.2 established the spherical s orbitals and penetration. 1.2.4 adds the five d orbitals and their
two angular nodes. 2.1 (VSEPR) and 2.3 (hybrid orbitals) use p directions to explain molecular
geometry, and 2.4 (MO theory) uses p–p overlap to build σ and π molecular orbitals.
