# Concept of energy levels
## 1. Prerequisites

1.1.2 for what a nuclide is and F.phys.1 for the photon relation E = hc/λ. The one new idea here
is that a bound particle can have only certain energies; the arithmetic is multiplication.

## 2. Hook

A neon sign glows orange-red. A sodium street lamp glows yellow. A hydrogen discharge tube glows
pinkish and, through a prism, splits into four visible lines at 656.3, 486.1, 434.0 and 410.2 nm
with dark gaps between them. A hot filament, by contrast, gives a smooth rainbow. Same physics,
different answer: the filament's electrons are free to take any energy, while the atoms' electrons
are locked into a fixed ladder and can only emit the gaps between rungs. Where this analogy
breaks: a real ladder's rungs are evenly spaced, while the atomic rungs crowd together as the
energy approaches zero.

## 3. Core idea

A bound electron has discrete allowed energies because it behaves as a standing wave, and a
standing wave must fit its boundary conditions. Hydrogen's levels are E_n = −13.6 Z²/n² eV, with
zero at the ionisation limit. A transition between two levels emits or absorbs one photon whose
energy equals the level difference, which is why atomic spectra are lines rather than bands and
why the lines of each element are a fingerprint.

## 4. Definition

An **energy level** is an allowed total energy of a bound electron, labelled by the principal
quantum number n = 1, 2, 3, …. The **ground state** is n = 1; an **excited state** is any level
above it. The **ionisation energy** is the energy needed to take an electron from its level to
E = 0, the continuum where it is free and at rest at infinity. The **Rydberg constant** for
hydrogen is R_H = 1.0967758 × 10^7 m^−1; the **Rydberg energy** is 13.598 eV, the magnitude of
the n = 1 level.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: why the energies are discrete.** A bound electron is described by a wavefunction that
must vanish at infinity and stay finite at the nucleus. Those boundary conditions admit only a
discrete set of wavelengths, exactly as a string clamped at both ends admits only its fundamental
and harmonics. Each allowed wavelength corresponds to an allowed energy. An electron with E > 0 is
not bound, so no boundary condition applies and its energy can be anything: a continuum.

**Step 2: the energies of hydrogen.** Solving the Coulomb problem gives

E_n = −13.6 Z²/n² eV, with Z = 1 for hydrogen:

E_1 = −13.598 eV, E_2 = −3.400 eV, E_3 = −1.511 eV, E_4 = −0.850 eV.

The spacing shrinks: 10.20 eV from n = 1 to 2, 1.889 eV from 2 to 3, 0.661 eV from 3 to 4. That
crowding is why spectral lines converge to a series limit instead of running to infinity.

**Step 3: where the formula comes from.** In SI the Coulomb force is e²/(4πε₀r²); write
k = 1/(4πε₀) so the algebra stays readable, and remember that every k carries that factor. Take a
circular orbit with Coulomb force providing the centripetal force, m v²/r = k e²/r², and impose the
wave condition m v r = n ħ:

v = nħ/(m r) ⇒ m n²ħ²/(m² r³) = k e²/r² ⇒ r_n = n² ħ²/(m k e²) = n² a_0.

The total energy is kinetic plus potential, E = ½ m v² − k e²/r, and the force balance makes
½ m v² = ½ k e²/r, so E = −k e²/(2r). Substituting r_n:

E_n = −(m k² e⁴)/(2 ħ²) · 1/n² = −13.6 eV/n².

Evaluating the constant with m = 9.109 × 10^−31 kg, e = 1.602 × 10^−19 C, ħ = 1.055 × 10^−34 J s
and k = 8.988 × 10^9 N m² C^−2 gives 2.18 × 10^−18 J, which is 13.6 eV. That evaluation is worth
doing once by hand so the number stops looking arbitrary.

For a hydrogen-like ion the nuclear charge is Ze, so every e² becomes Z e² and the energy gains a
factor Z². The Bohr model is not the modern picture — it cannot handle helium, fine structure or
orbital shapes — but for one-electron systems its energies are exactly right, which is why the
formula survives.

**Step 4: transitions.** A photon carries energy hν = hc/λ, and an electron changing levels must
absorb or emit exactly the difference:

hc/λ = E_upper − E_lower = 13.6 Z² (1/n_lower² − 1/n_upper²) eV.

Dividing by hc and evaluating the constant gives the Rydberg formula

1/λ = R (1/n_1² − 1/n_2²), with n_2 > n_1.

Emission happens when the electron falls (photon out); absorption when it climbs (photon in, at the
same wavelength). The spectrum is therefore the same set of wavelengths seen in emission and in
absorption, which is a strong test of the model.

**Step 5: the series.** n_1 fixes the series. Lyman (n_1 = 1) is ultraviolet, Balmer (n_1 = 2)
visible, Paschen (n_1 = 3) infrared. Each series converges to a limit at n_2 → ∞, the energy needed
to ionise from that lower level: for Balmer the limit is 13.6/4 = 3.40 eV, λ = 364.7 nm.

**Step 6: using the constant correctly.** hc = 1239.842 eV nm converts an energy difference in eV
directly to a wavelength in nm; hc = 1.986 × 10^−25 J m does the same in SI. Both are exact
consequences of h and c, so mixing them up shows as a wavelength off by 10^9, not a subtle error.
For accuracy better than 0.1 %, use R_H = 1.0967758 × 10^7 m^−1 for hydrogen: the infinite-mass
Rydberg constant 1.097373 × 10^7 m^−1 differs because the nucleus recoils slightly.

The ladder is drawn at the computed energies, not to an artist's spacing, and each arrow's label is
computed from the same two levels it connects. The shrinking gaps between rungs and the convergence
toward zero are the visual content: the higher the level, the closer the next one.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The formula assumes one electron and a fixed nucleus: it ignores electron–electron repulsion,
relativistic corrections (important for inner electrons of heavy atoms), spin–orbit coupling (which
splits lines into fine structure) and the finite nuclear mass (a 0.05 % effect for hydrogen). It
also assumes the electron is non-relativistic and that the photon carries all the transition
energy; in reality the atom recoils slightly. For multi-electron atoms the same ladder picture is
an approximation: energy depends on both n and l, which is why 2s and 2p differ in lithium.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** Hydrogen, transitions 3 → 2, 2 → 1 and the ionisation from n = 2. Use R_H =
1.0967758 × 10^7 m^−1 and hc = 1239.842 eV nm.

**Hanapin.** The wavelength of each transition, the series each belongs to, and whether each is
visible.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: identify n_1 (the lower level, which names the series) before substituting.

3 → 2 (Balmer, Hα):

1/λ = 1.0967758 × 10^7 (1/4 − 1/9) = 1.0967758 × 10^7 × 0.138889
    = 1.52330 × 10^6 m^−1
λ = 6.5647 × 10^−7 m = 656.5 nm in vacuum, 656.3 nm in air.

2 → 1 (Lyman, Lyα):

1/λ = 1.0967758 × 10^7 (1 − 1/4) = 8.22582 × 10^6 m^−1
λ = 1.2157 × 10^−7 m = 121.6 nm, ultraviolet.

Ionisation from n = 2: the electron goes to E = 0, so ΔE = 3.400 eV and

λ = 1239.842/3.400 = 364.7 nm,

the Balmer limit. Check with the constants: 13.598/4 = 3.3995 eV, giving 364.7 nm.

**Sagot.** Hα 656.3 nm (visible), Lyα 121.6 nm (ultraviolet), Balmer limit 364.7 nm. Every Balmer
line lies between 364.7 and 656.3 nm.

## 9. Traps

1. Writing E_n = +13.6/n² eV. The negative sign encodes binding: E = 0 is free and at rest, so
   bound states must be below zero. A positive E_1 would mean the electron is unbound.
2. Using n = 1 for both levels, giving division by zero. The two levels in a transition are
   different; the lower one names the series.
3. Mixing up n_1 and n_2 in the Rydberg formula, which gives a negative 1/λ. Order them so the
   bracket is positive.
4. Treating the series limit as a line. It is the convergence point at n → ∞, where the photon has
   exactly the ionisation energy of that lower level.
5. Comparing 656.3 nm with 656.1 nm and calling the 0.2 nm gap the reduced-mass correction:
   those are different scales. In vacuum, R_H gives 656.47 nm and R∞ gives 656.11 nm, a
   0.36 nm gap from the reduced mass; the 656.3 nm line is the air wavelength, and the
   air-vacuum shift (~0.19 nm) is a separate correction. The reduced-mass shift is why the
   same transition has a slightly different wavelength in deuterium.
6. Confusing excitation with ionisation. Excitation moves an electron between bound levels;
   ionisation removes it to E = 0 and beyond.

## 10. Check questions

1. Find the wavelength of the 4 → 2 transition and name the series.
   1/λ = R(1/4 − 1/16) = R(0.1875) = 2.0565 × 10^6 m^−1, λ = 486.3 nm (vacuum), 486.1 nm in air;
   Balmer, Hβ, blue-green.
2. What energy is needed to ionise hydrogen from n = 1, and from n = 3?
   13.598 eV from n = 1; 13.598/9 = 1.511 eV from n = 3. The higher the starting level, the less
   energy is needed.
3. Why does the Balmer series have both a shortest and a longest wavelength?
   The longest comes from the smallest gap, 3 → 2, at 656.3 nm; the shortest comes from the largest
   gap, n = ∞ → 2, at 364.7 nm. Both ends exist because the lower level is fixed at n = 2 while the
   upper level runs over 3, 4, 5, …, ∞.
4. A hydrogen atom absorbs a 121.6 nm photon. What happens?
   121.6 nm is Lyα, 10.20 eV, exactly the 1 → 2 gap. The electron is excited to n = 2 and the
   photon is gone; the atom will re-emit at 121.6 nm or cascade through 656.3 nm.
