# Square of the wave function and probability
## 1. Prerequisites

1.2.5 supplies ψ_1s and its energy; 1.2.2 and 1.2.3 give the orbital pictures this card turns
into numbers. F.math.2 supplies the exponential, the limit idea and the unit algebra. It does
**not** cover integration by parts, so Step 3 derives that rule from the product rule and
evaluates both integrals on the page; nothing is imported silently.

## 2. Hook

Ask where the 1s electron of a hydrogen atom is and the honest answer is "everywhere, with
different odds". The density is highest exactly at the nucleus, yet the most likely distance
from the nucleus is 52.9 pm. Both statements are true, and the reason is that a shell of radius
r has more room in it than a shell of radius r − dr. Where this analogy breaks: a probability
distribution is not ignorance about a definite position that the electron secretly has. Quantum
mechanics gives the electron no definite position before a measurement, so "it is somewhere, we
just do not know where" is not what the distribution means.

## 3. Core idea

|ψ|² is a probability per unit volume, so a probability needs a volume: P = |ψ|²dV. For a
spherical atom the natural volume is a thin shell, dV = 4πr²dr, which gives the radial
distribution P(r) = 4πr²|ψ|². The density peaks at the nucleus; the radial distribution peaks
at a₀ for 1s. Every probability question on this card is one integral of one of those two
functions over the right region.

## 4. Definition

**Probability density** ρ(r) = |ψ(r)|²: probability per unit volume, units m^−3. **Radial
distribution function** P(r) = 4πr²|ψ(r)|², normalised so that ∫₀^∞ P(r)dr = 1.
**Expectation value** of an observable A is ⟨A⟩ = ∫ψ*Âψ dV, where ψ* is the complex conjugate
and Â is the operator for A — multiplication by x for position, a derivative for momentum — so
the order of ψ*, Â and ψ matters when two operators do not commute. **Most probable radius**
r_mp is the radius where P(r) is largest, that is dP/dr = 0. **Mean radius** is ⟨r⟩ =
∫rP(r)dr. The two are different numbers: 52.9 pm and 79.35 pm for hydrogen 1s.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: why a density is not a probability.** A point has zero volume, and a continuous
density multiplied by zero volume is zero, so the probability of finding the electron at one
exact point is zero. Only regions have non-zero probability. "The electron is at r = a₀" is
shorthand for "in a thin shell around a₀".

**Step 2: the shell volume.** A sphere of radius r has volume 4πr³/3, so the shell between r and
r + dr holds

V_shell = (4π/3)[(r + dr)³ − r³] = (4π/3)[3r²dr + 3r(dr)² + (dr)³].

Dividing by dr and letting dr → 0 leaves only the first-order term, so dV = 4πr²dr. That r² is
the whole story: near the nucleus the shell volume vanishes, so even a large density gives a
small probability.

**Step 3: from ψ to P(r ≤ R), every step.** With ψ_1s = (1/√π)(Z/a₀)^(3/2)e^(−Zr/a₀), the
density is |ψ|² = (1/π)(Z/a₀)³e^(−2Zr/a₀), and

4πr²|ψ|²dr = 4π r² (1/π)(Z/a₀)³ e^(−2Zr/a₀) dr = 4 (Z/a₀)³ r² e^(−2Zr/a₀) dr.

Substitute x = Zr/a₀, so r = a₀x/Z and dr = (a₀/Z)dx. The three powers of length cancel exactly,
(Z/a₀)³ · (a₀/Z)² · (a₀/Z) = 1, leaving

P(r ≤ R) = 4∫₀^X x²e^(−2x)dx, with X = ZR/a₀.

That cancellation is the reason P(r ≤ R) depends only on the ratio R/a₀.

Two integrals remain. First the substitution u = 2x, with x = u/2, dx = du/2 and upper limit
2X:

4∫x²e^(−2x)dx = 4∫(u²/4)e^(−u)(du/2) = (1/2)∫u²e^(−u)du.

Second, the by-parts rule itself, derived rather than quoted. The product rule is
d(fg)/du = f′g + fg′; integrating both sides and rearranging gives ∫f′g du = fg − ∫fg′du. Apply
it with f′ = e^(−u) (so f = −e^(−u)) and g = u² (so g′ = 2u):

∫u²e^(−u)du = −u²e^(−u) + 2∫ue^(−u)du.

Apply it again with f′ = e^(−u) and g = u:

∫ue^(−u)du = −ue^(−u) + ∫e^(−u)du = −ue^(−u) − e^(−u).

Substituting the second result into the first:

<br />∫u²e^(−u)du = −e^(−u)(u² + 2u + 2) + C.<br />

Evaluate between u = 0 and u = U = 2X; the bracket at u = 0 equals 2, so the definite integral
is 2 − e^(−U)(U² + 2U + 2). Multiply by the 1/2 from the substitution and put U = 2X:

<br />P(r ≤ R) = 1 − e^(−2x)(1 + 2x + 2x²), x = R/a₀.<br />

Limits check it: x = 0 gives 1 − 1 = 0, and x → ∞ gives 1 − 0 = 1, as a probability must.
**Step 4: three numbers, each derived.** (a) Most probable radius. With P(x) = 4x²e^(−2x),

dP/dx = 8x e^(−2x) − 8x²e^(−2x) = 8x(1 − x)e^(−2x),

which vanishes at x = 0, where P = 0, and at x = 1, where P = 4e^−2 = 0.5413 is largest. So
r_mp = a₀ = 52.9 pm for hydrogen.

(b) Mean radius. ⟨r⟩ = ∫₀^∞ rP(r)dr = 4(a₀/Z)∫₀^∞ x³e^(−2x)dx. With u = 2x,
x³dx = u³du/16, so ⟨r⟩ = (a₀/4Z)∫₀^∞ u³e^(−u)du. The same by-parts passes give
∫u³e^(−u)du = −e^(−u)(u³ + 3u² + 6u + 6) + C, so the integral from 0 to ∞ is 6, since every
term of the boundary value carries e^(−u). Hence ⟨r⟩ = 6a₀/4Z = 1.5a₀/Z = 79.35 pm for
hydrogen: the peak sits at a₀ and the average at 1.5a₀, pulled outward by the long tail.

(c) Two fractions. P(r ≤ a₀) = 1 − e^−2(1 + 2 + 2) = 1 − 0.13534 × 5 = 0.3233, and
P(r ≤ 2a₀) = 1 − e^−4(1 + 4 + 8) = 1 − 0.018316 × 13 = 0.7619.

**Step 5: nodes and probability.** At a node ψ = 0, so the density is zero there. For 2s the
radial factor is (2 − r/a₀)e^(−r/2a₀), which vanishes at r = 2a₀ = 105.8 pm: no electron is
found exactly on that sphere, and the density is non-zero on both sides of it. "The electron
cannot cross the node" is not a statement quantum mechanics makes; there is no trajectory to
cross anything.

**Step 6: what a measurement gives.** Each measurement finds the electron at one point; the
distribution is the statistics of many identical preparations. The "cloud" is a summary of
those statistics, not a physical object with a soft edge.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The formulas assume a hydrogenic 1s orbital, a point nucleus and no electron correlation. For
multi-electron atoms the radial function changes and the numbers above no longer apply exactly,
though the density-versus-distribution distinction does. Relativistic effects contract s
orbitals near heavy nuclei, raising the density at the nucleus and changing hyperfine couplings.
P(r)dr is a probability only for a shell thin enough that P is nearly constant across it, which
is why dV was expanded to first order in Step 2. Probability statements are about ensembles: a
single atom gives one outcome, not a fraction of one.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** A hydrogen atom in its 1s state, a₀ = 52.9 pm, and
P(r ≤ R) = 1 − e^(−2x)(1 + 2x + 2x²) with x = R/a₀.

**Hanapin.** (a) the probability of finding the electron within a₀; (b) within 2a₀; (c) beyond
2a₀; (d) the most probable and the mean radius, and which is larger.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: the only variable is x = R/a₀, so convert each radius to x before touching
the exponential. For (a) x = 1: P = 1 − e^−2(1 + 2 + 2) = 1 − 0.13534 × 5 = 1 − 0.67668 =
0.3233. For (b) x = 2: P = 1 − e^−4(1 + 4 + 8) = 1 − 0.018316 × 13 = 1 − 0.23810 = 0.7619. For
(c) the complement: 1 − 0.7619 = 0.2381. Sanity check: inside a₀, a₀ to 2a₀, and beyond 2a₀ give
0.3233 + 0.4386 + 0.2381 = 1.0000. For (d) r_mp comes from dP/dx = 8x(1 − x)e^(−2x) = 0, so
x = 1 and r_mp = 52.9 pm; ⟨r⟩ = 1.5a₀ = 1.5 × 52.9 pm = 79.35 pm. The mean is larger, because
the long tail at large r counts fully in the average but contributes little area near the peak.

**Sagot.** (a) 32.3 % within a₀; (b) 76.2 % within 2a₀; (c) 23.8 % beyond 2a₀; (d) r_mp =
52.9 pm, ⟨r⟩ = 79.35 pm, so the mean exceeds the most probable radius.

## 9. Traps

1. Reporting |ψ|² as a probability. It is a density: it needs a volume, and its units are m^−3.
2. Using dV = dr instead of 4πr²dr for a spherical atom. That removes the r², makes the density
   largest at the nucleus the "most probable radius", and lowkey destroys the whole card.
3. Confusing the most probable radius with the mean: 52.9 pm versus 79.35 pm for 1s.
4. Forgetting to square ψ before integrating, or squaring the normalisation constant twice.
5. Thinking a node is a barrier. The density is zero there, and the electron has no trajectory.
6. Expecting P(r ≤ a₀) = 0.5 because a₀ is the peak. The distribution is asymmetric with a long
   tail, so the point that splits the probability in half is not the peak.
7. Writing ⟨p⟩ as ∫ψ*pψ dV with a number instead of an operator. For momentum the operator is
   −iħ d/dx, and the hat is not decoration.

## 10. Check questions

1. Why is the probability of finding a 1s electron exactly at the nucleus zero even though the
   density there is maximal?
   A point has zero volume, and density times zero volume is zero. Only finite regions have
   non-zero probability.
2. What fraction of 1s probability lies outside 2a₀?
   1 − 0.7619 = 0.2381, about 23.8 %.
3. For a 1s electron, which is larger, the most probable radius or the mean radius, and why?
   The mean, 1.5a₀ = 79.35 pm against a₀ = 52.9 pm, because the long tail at large r pulls the
   average outward.
4. Where is the 2s radial distribution zero, and what does that mean physically?
   At the node r = 2a₀ = 105.8 pm; the density is zero on that sphere, with non-zero density on
   both sides of it.
5. Why does the definition of ⟨A⟩ put the operator between ψ* and ψ rather than after ψ?
   Because for non-commuting operators such as x and p the order changes the integral; Â must
   act on ψ before the product is integrated, which is what ⟨A⟩ = ∫ψ*Âψ dV encodes.
