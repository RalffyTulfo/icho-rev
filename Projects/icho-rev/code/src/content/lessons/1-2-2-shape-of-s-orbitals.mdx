# Shape of s-orbitals
## 1. Prerequisites

1.2.1 for energy levels and the meaning of n, plus 1.2.1's warning that an orbital is a
wavefunction and not a path.

## 2. Hook

Every picture of an atom in a textbook is a lie in one specific way: it draws electrons as dots on
rings. The honest picture for an s electron is a sphere with no preferred direction at all — rotate
the atom and nothing changes — and for the 2s orbital that sphere has a hollow shell inside it
where the electron is never found. Where this analogy breaks: an onion's layers are physical
surfaces you could peel, while the "shell" here is a surface where a mathematical function passes
through zero, with electron density on both sides of it.

## 3. Core idea

An s orbital has zero orbital angular momentum (l = 0), so its wavefunction depends only on r and
its shape is a sphere. Its radial part has n − 1 nodes, which is why 1s has none, 2s has one and
3s has two. The density |ψ|² is largest at the nucleus, but the radial distribution 4πr²|ψ|² — the
curve you actually plot — peaks away from it, because the volume of a thin shell shrinks to zero
at the centre.

## 4. Definition

An **s orbital** is a one-electron state with l = 0 and m_l = 0. It has no angular node and n − 1
radial nodes; the angular part is a constant, so the shape is spherically symmetric. A **radial
node** is a spherical surface at radius r_node where the radial function R(r) = 0. The **radial
distribution function** is P(r) = 4πr²|R(r)|², normalised so that ∫P dr = 1. **Penetration** is the
extent to which an orbital's density lies close to the nucleus, inside the core electrons; it is why
2s lies below 2p in multi-electron atoms.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: why the shape is a sphere.** With l = 0 the angular equation has a constant solution, so
ψ(r, θ, φ) = R(r) · constant. Nothing depends on direction, so every rotation leaves the orbital
unchanged. That is the entire content of "s orbitals are spherical"; it is a symmetry statement,
not a drawing convention.

**Step 2: the radial functions.** For hydrogen, with σ = Zr/a₀,

R_1s ∝ e^(−σ), R_2s ∝ (2 − σ)e^(−σ/2), R_3s ∝ (27 − 18σ + 2σ²)e^(−σ/3).

Count the sign changes in the bracket: 1s has none, 2s has one, 3s has two, matching n − 1. Each
zero of the bracket is a radial node, at σ = 2 for 2s, so r_node = 2a₀/Z. For hydrogen that is
105.8 pm; for He⁺ it is 52.9 pm, because the larger nuclear charge pulls the whole orbital inward.

**Step 3: density versus distribution.** |ψ|² is largest at r = 0 for every s orbital: the
exponential is at its maximum there. But the probability of finding the electron in a thin shell
between r and r + dr is 4πr²|ψ|² dr, and the r² factor beats the exponential near the origin. For
1s the product peaks at r = a₀ = 52.9 pm. For 2s it has two maxima, at about 0.76a₀ and
5.24a₀, one inside the node and one outside, with the outer one taller; the node is a zero between them.

**Step 4: mean radii.** The expectation value for a hydrogen-like atom is

⟨r⟩ = (a₀/2Z)(3n² − l(l + 1)).

Substituting Z = 1: 1s gives (a₀/2)(3) = 1.5a₀; 2s gives (a₀/2)(12) = 6a₀; 2p gives (a₀/2)(10) =
5a₀. In picometres those are 79.4, 317 and 264 pm, so the 2s electron is on average further out than
the 2p electron despite the same n. Note 2s is larger than 2p on average even though both have n = 2: the 2s has a radial
node and an inner lobe that reaches close to the nucleus, and reaching close costs average distance.
That inner lobe is the penetration that matters in multi-electron atoms.

**Step 5: why penetration changes the energy order.** In hydrogen all orbitals with the same n are
degenerate. In lithium the 2s electron spends part of its time inside the 1s core, so it feels more
of the full nuclear charge and less shielding; 2p has a node at the nucleus and almost no core
penetration, so it is screened more. Result: E(2s) &lt; E(2p), which is why the aufbau order fills 2s
before 2p and why the periodic table's structure exists at all. The ordering is not a postulate; it
is a consequence of the radial shapes on this card.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The functions are hydrogenic: one electron, point nucleus, no electron–electron repulsion. For
many-electron atoms the shapes are close but the energies are not, and the radial functions are only
approximations from self-consistent field methods. The picture also hides spin entirely, and it
treats the electron as a wave with no internal structure. Finally, "the electron is in the sphere"
is loose language: the orbital is a probability amplitude, and one measurement finds the electron at
one point, not smeared over the shell.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** The hydrogen 2s radial function R_2s ∝ (2 − Zr/a₀)e^(−Zr/2a₀), with a₀ = 52.9 pm.

**Hanapin.** The radius of the radial node for H and for He⁺, the number of radial nodes in 3s, and
the most probable radius for the 1s electron.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: nodes come from zeros of the radial function, not from zeros of the density.
Here they coincide, because squaring a zero gives zero, but the distinction matters when you are
asked for a sign change.

Set the bracket to zero: 2 − Zr/a₀ = 0, so r = 2a₀/Z.

For H, Z = 1: r_node = 2 × 52.9 = 105.8 pm.
For He⁺, Z = 2: r_node = 105.8/2 = 52.9 pm.

3s: n − 1 = 2 radial nodes. Its bracket 27 − 18σ + 2σ² has two positive roots, consistent with the
count without solving it.

1s most probable radius: maximise 4πr²e^(−2Zr/a₀); the derivative gives r = a₀/Z, so 52.9 pm for H.
The mean is 1.5a₀ = 79.4 pm, which is a different question with a different answer.

**Sagot.** 105.8 pm for H, 52.9 pm for He⁺, two radial nodes in 3s, and 52.9 pm for the most
probable 1s radius.

## 9. Traps

1. Saying the 2s node is at a₀. It is at 2a₀ = 105.8 pm; a₀ is where the 1s radial distribution
   peaks.
2. Confusing |ψ|² with the radial distribution. The density is largest at the nucleus; the
   distribution is zero there and peaks at a₀.
3. Drawing an s orbital as a ring or a circle in one plane. It is a sphere; any planar drawing is a
   cross-section.
4. Claiming 2s is higher in energy than 2p in every atom. In hydrogen they are degenerate; in
   multi-electron atoms 2s is lower because of penetration.
5. Treating the node as a place the electron "cannot pass through". The electron is not travelling;
   the node is a surface where the probability density is zero, with density on both sides.
6. Reporting ⟨r⟩ = a₀ for 1s. a₀ is the most probable radius; the mean is 1.5a₀.

## 10. Check questions

1. How many radial and angular nodes does 3s have?
   Two radial (n − 1 = 2), zero angular (l = 0). Total two, which equals n − 1.
2. For hydrogen, is the electron more likely to be found at r = 0 or at r = a₀?
   The density is highest at r = 0, but the probability of a shell is highest at a₀. The rule: for a
   point, quote the density; for a distance from the nucleus, quote the radial distribution, which is
   what "most probable radius" means in every exam question. So the answer here is a₀ = 52.9 pm, and
   the r = 0 statement is a different, less useful fact.
3. Why is E(2s) below E(2p) in lithium?
   The 2s electron penetrates the 1s core, feels a larger effective nuclear charge and is stabilised;
   2p has a node at the nucleus, penetrates less and is screened more.
4. What is the mean radius of a 2s electron in hydrogen, and how does it compare with 2p?
   6a₀ for 2s versus 5a₀ for 2p; the 2s is larger on average despite the same n.
