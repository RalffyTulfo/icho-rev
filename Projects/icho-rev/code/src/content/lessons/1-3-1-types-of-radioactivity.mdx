# Types of radioactivity
## 1. Prerequisites

1.1.1 — the meaning of Z, A and N, and the fact that an ion's charge does not change its nucleus.

## 2. Hook

Four ways for a nucleus to shed energy, and they are told apart by what they change. Alpha takes two
protons and two neutrons with it; beta-minus turns a neutron into a proton and spits out an electron;
beta-plus and electron capture do the reverse; gamma changes nothing about the nucleus's composition,
only its excitation. A decay scheme is a bookkeeping puzzle in which A and Z must balance, and once
you can balance them the names are just labels for the four moves. Where this analogy breaks: a
bookkeeping move is instantaneous in the notation, while real decays take time and can leave the
daughter in an excited state that emits later.

## 3. Core idea

Every radioactive decay conserves nucleon number A and charge Z. Alpha lowers A by 4 and Z by 2;
beta-minus raises Z by 1 and leaves A; beta-plus and electron capture lower Z by 1 and leave A;
gamma changes neither. The type of decay is therefore determined by the changes it produces, and a
chain is solved by counting alphas from the A difference and betas from the Z difference.

## 4. Definition

**Radioactivity** is the spontaneous emission of particles or photons by an unstable nucleus. An
**alpha particle** is a helium-4 nucleus (A = 4, Z = 2). A **beta-minus** particle is an electron
emitted when a neutron converts to a proton, accompanied by an antineutrino. A **beta-plus** particle
is a positron, emitted when a proton converts to a neutron, accompanied by a neutrino. **Electron
capture** is the absorption of an inner-shell electron in the same proton-to-neutron conversion. A
**gamma ray** is a photon emitted by an excited nucleus; it carries no A and no Z.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: conservation first.** Write the equation with A as superscripts and Z as subscripts on
both sides. Nucleon number and charge must each balance. Everything else — the name of the decay, the
particle emitted, the daughter element — follows.

**Step 2: alpha.** ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. A: 238 = 234 + 4; Z: 92 = 90 + 2. The daughter is two
places lower in the periodic table.

**Step 3: beta-minus.** ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̄. A: 14 = 14; Z: 6 = 7 − 1, using the electron's charge
of −1. A neutron became a proton, so the element moves one place up. The antineutrino is required to
conserve energy and momentum in a three-body decay.

The electron comes from the nucleus, not from the electron cloud: a neutron has changed into a
proton, and the emitted electron is created in that change. This is why the atomic number rises
even though the cloud has lost nothing.

**Step 4: beta-plus and electron capture.** ²²₁₁Na → ²²₁₀Ne + e⁺ + ν, and ⁵⁵₂₆Fe + e⁻ → ⁵⁵₂₅Mn + ν.
Both change Z by −1 and A by 0. Beta-plus needs at least 1.022 MeV of available energy to create the
positron pair; below that threshold, electron capture is the only route.

**Step 5: gamma.** ⁶⁰₂₇Co* → ⁶⁰₂₇Co + γ. Neither A nor Z changes, so the isotope is unchanged. Gamma
usually follows an alpha or beta decay that left the daughter excited, and the metastable label m
marks a long-lived excited state, as in ⁹⁹ᵐTc.

**Step 6: penetration follows charge and mass.** Alpha is heavy and 2+, ionises densely and stops in
paper or a few centimetres of air. Beta is light and singly charged, stopped by a few millimetres of
aluminium. Gamma is uncharged and attenuated gradually, needing dense shielding such as lead.

## 6. Diagram

Draw a decay-scheme chart with Z on the vertical axis and N on the horizontal axis. Mark a parent
nuclide and draw four arrows from it: alpha as a diagonal step two left and two down; beta-minus as
one step up; beta-plus and electron capture as one step down; gamma as no movement, drawn as a
wavy vertical arrow at the same point. Label each arrow with the change in (A, Z).

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The bookkeeping says what is possible, not what is likely: a balanced equation does not tell you the
branching ratio or the half-life. Real decays can populate excited states, so gamma emission is often
part of a sequence rather than an alternative. The neutrino is omitted in most school equations but
is physically required. And "alpha, beta, gamma" is not exhaustive: spontaneous fission, proton
emission and cluster decay exist, though they lie outside this syllabus item.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** ²²⁶₈₈Ra decays by alpha; ³²₁₅P decays by beta-minus; ¹⁸₉F decays by beta-plus.

**Hanapin.** The daughter nuclide of each, and the change in A and Z.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: write the conservation equations before naming any element.

Radium: A: 226 − 4 = 222; Z: 88 − 2 = 86. Element 86 is radon, so ²²²₈₆Rn + α.

Phosphorus: A stays 32; Z: 15 + 1 = 16, which is sulfur, so ³²₁₆S + e⁻ + ν̄.

Fluorine: A stays 18; Z: 9 − 1 = 8, which is oxygen, so ¹⁸₈O + e⁺ + ν.

Check each: superscripts and subscripts balance, and the daughter's Z matches the element symbol.

**Sagot.** ²²²₈₆Rn (A −4, Z −2); ³²₁₆S (A 0, Z +1); ¹⁸₈O (A 0, Z −1).

## 9. Traps

1. Saying gamma changes the isotope. It changes neither A nor Z; it only relaxes an excited nuclear
   state.
2. Writing beta-minus as Z − 1. A neutron becomes a proton, so Z rises by one.
3. Mixing up beta-plus and electron capture: both give Z − 1, but the emitted particle differs, and
   beta-plus needs 1.022 MeV.
4. Forgetting the neutrino or antineutrino when the question asks what else is emitted.
5. Treating alpha as "helium given off" without subtracting A and Z in the equation.
6. Assuming the daughter of an alpha decay is an isotope of the parent. It is a different element,
   two places lower.

## 10. Check questions

1. ²¹⁰₈₄Po emits an alpha. What is the daughter?
   A: 206, Z: 82, so ²⁰⁶₈₂Pb. Check: 206 + 4 = 210 and 82 + 2 = 84.
2. Which decay leaves A unchanged and raises Z by one?
   Beta-minus. Beta-plus and electron capture lower Z; alpha lowers both.
3. Why is a gamma often emitted after a beta decay?
   The beta process can leave the daughter nucleus in an excited state, which relaxes by emitting a
   photon without changing A or Z.
4. Why does alpha radiation stop in paper while gamma needs lead?
   Alpha is heavy and doubly charged, so it deposits energy in a very short distance; gamma is
   uncharged and interacts only probabilistically.
