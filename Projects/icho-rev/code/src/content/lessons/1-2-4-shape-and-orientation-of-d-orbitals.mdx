# Shape and orientation of d-orbitals
## 1. Prerequisites

1.2.2 and 1.2.3 — the s and p shapes, the node formulas, and the distinction between an orbital and
a subshell. This card assumes you can already count nodes from n and l.

## 2. Hook

Four of the five d orbitals look like a four-leaf clover with the leaves between or along the axes.
The fifth looks wrong: two lobes on the z-axis and a doughnut around the middle. Students draw that
one as four lobes and lose the mark, not because the drawing is untidy but because the shape is the
information. Where this analogy breaks: a clover has physical leaves, while these are isosurfaces of
equal probability chosen at an arbitrary cutoff, and the sign of the wavefunction alternates between
lobes.

## 3. Core idea

A d orbital has l = 2: two angular nodes and n − 3 radial nodes, so 3d has none, 4d has one and 5d
has two. There are five of them, 2(2l + 1) = 10 electrons per subshell, and they are degenerate only
in the isolated-atom central field. Four have two nodal planes; d_z² has two nodal cones, which is
why its picture is different rather than badly drawn.

## 4. Definition

A **d orbital** is a one-electron state with l = 2, m_l = −2 … +2. The five real functions are d_xy,
d_xz, d_yz, d_x²−y² and d_z². A **nodal cone** is the angular surface of d_z² where the angular
function vanishes. **Crystal-field splitting** is the lifting of the fivefold degeneracy by an
environment of ligands, parametrised by Δ, with Δ = 10 Dq, so the two symbols name the same splitting.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: the angular functions.** In Cartesian form the five are proportional to xy, xz, yz,
x² − y², and 2z² − x² − y². Each vanishes where its expression is zero: xy = 0 on the two planes
x = 0 and y = 0, and similarly for the other cross terms. That is where the two nodal planes of
d_xy, d_xz, d_yz and d_x²−y² come from. One bookkeeping point that trips people up: the
normalised angular functions Y_2,m carry a factor 1/r², so it is r²Y_2,m — not Y_2,m — that
equals those polynomials. Writing "the angular function is xy" is shorthand for "the angular
part is proportional to xy/r²"; the r² is restored when the radial factor comes back in.

**Step 2: why d_z² is different.** Set 2z² − x² − y² = 0. In spherical coordinates
z = r cos θ and x² + y² = r² sin²θ, so

2z² − x² − y² = r²(2cos²θ − sin²θ).

Replace sin²θ by 1 − cos²θ:

2cos²θ − (1 − cos²θ) = 3cos²θ − 1.

So the angular zero condition is 3cos²θ − 1 = 0, i.e. cos θ = ±1/√3 and θ = 54.7° or 125.3°.
(Dropping the r² and stopping at 2cos²θ − 1 = 0 is the common slip: that equation has no root at
cos θ = ±1/√3, and it would put the zeros in the wrong place.) The zeros are two cones opening
along ±z, not planes. The function keeps one sign inside the cones (the lobes) and the opposite
sign in the equatorial torus, which is why the torus is drawn with the opposite shading.

**Step 3: radial nodes.** Radial nodes = n − l − 1 = n − 3. 3d: 0. 4d: 1, a spherical surface where
the whole four-lobed pattern changes sign. 5d: 2. Total nodes = n − 1: 2, 3, 4 respectively, and the
angular contribution is always 2.

**Step 4: why five, and why 10 electrons.** m_l takes five values, −2 to +2, so five orbitals; each
takes two spins, so ten electrons. The d block of the periodic table is ten columns wide for the same
reason the p block is six and the f block fourteen.

**Step 5: degeneracy is conditional.** In hydrogen all five have the same energy, because the only
thing the energy depends on is n. In an octahedral complex, measured from the barycentre (weighted mean of the five, set to zero), the two axial-pointing orbitals (d_z² and
d_x²−y²) rise by +0.6Δ and the three between-axis orbitals (d_xy, d_xz, d_yz) fall by −0.4Δ, keeping
the barycentre fixed: 2(0.6Δ) + 3(−0.4Δ) = 0. That splitting, not the shapes alone, is what makes
transition-metal chemistry colourful and magnetic.

**Step 6: why 4s fills before 3d without contradicting this.** The filling order is a bookkeeping
heuristic, not an energy-level diagram, and it is easy to garble. The honest statement has two
parts. (a) For neutral potassium and calcium (Z = 19, 20) the 4s orbital is lower in energy than
3d, because a 4s function penetrates the argon core and samples more nuclear charge than a 3d
function does; hence [Ar]4s¹ and [Ar]4s². (b) From scandium (Z = 21) onward the 3d orbital lies
below 4s in the neutral atom, yet the 4s electrons remain, because the total energy of the atom
includes electron–electron repulsion and the d⁰s² to d^n s² arrangement is the lower one. What
changed between (a) and (b) is Z, not a rule about ions of the previous row: K and Ca are neutral
period-4 atoms, not an ion of period 3. Quote configurations, not slogans.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The shapes are isosurfaces at a chosen probability; a different cutoff gives a different picture of
the same orbital. The real functions are one basis among infinitely many, and the complex m_l
functions are equally valid. The fivefold degeneracy holds only in a central field, so any statement
about d-orbital energies outside an isolated atom needs the environment specified. Finally, the
shapes say nothing about spin or about electron–electron repulsion, which is why multi-electron d
configurations need Hund's rules on top.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** The five 3d orbitals, and an octahedral complex with splitting Δ = 20 000 cm⁻¹.

**Hanapin.** The radial and angular node counts for 3d and 4d, the number of electrons a d subshell
holds, and the energy of each set in the complex relative to the barycentre.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: n and l first. 3d has n = 3, l = 2, so radial = 3 − 2 − 1 = 0 and angular = 2;
total 2 = n − 1. 4d has n = 4, l = 2, so radial = 1 and angular = 2; total 3.

Electrons: 2(2l + 1) = 2(5) = 10, five orbitals times two spins.

Octahedral energies relative to the barycentre: the e_g set (d_z², d_x²−y²) at +0.6Δ = +12 000 cm⁻¹,
the t_2g set (d_xy, d_xz, d_yz) at −0.4Δ = −8000 cm⁻¹. Check the weighted mean:
[2(12 000) + 3(−8000)]/5 = (24 000 − 24 000)/5 = 0, so the barycentre is unmoved.

**Sagot.** 3d: 0 radial, 2 angular. 4d: 1 radial, 2 angular. Ten electrons per d subshell. In the
complex, e_g at +12 000 cm⁻¹ and t_2g at −8000 cm⁻¹.

## 9. Traps

1. Drawing d_z² as four lobes. It is two lobes plus a torus; the torus is the part with the opposite
   sign.
2. Saying d orbitals have two nodal planes. Four do; d_z² has two nodal cones.
3. "A d orbital holds 10 electrons." The subshell holds 10; one orbital holds 2.
4. Asserting the five are degenerate in every atom. Only in the isolated central field.
5. Forgetting the radial node in 4d. It is a sphere cutting the whole shape, not an extra plane.
6. Using the shape to explain why 4s fills before 3d. The explanation is total energy, not shape.

## 10. Check questions

1. How many radial nodes does 5d have, and how many angular nodes?
   Radial = 5 − 2 − 1 = 2; angular = 2; total 4 = n − 1.
2. Why does d_z² have cones rather than planes as its angular nodes?
   Because its angular function is proportional to 3cos²θ − 1, whose zeros are at fixed θ (54.7°
   and 125.3°), which traces cones; a plane would require a zero at fixed φ or at a fixed
   Cartesian coordinate. Using 2cos²θ − 1 here is the classic slip: it is not the angular part of
   2z² − x² − y².
3. An octahedral complex has Δ = 18 000 cm⁻¹. Where are the two sets?
   e_g at +0.6Δ = +10 800 cm⁻¹; t_2g at −0.4Δ = −7200 cm⁻¹; check 2(10 800) = 3(7200).
4. How many electrons fit in the 4f subshell, and how many orbitals is that?
   14 electrons in 7 orbitals, from 2(2l + 1) with l = 3.
