# Understanding the simplest Schrodinger equation
## 1. Prerequisites

1.2.1 for the empirical level ladder and for the idea that spectra come in lines. **No calculus
prerequisite:** this card states the two derivatives it uses, and it says what a boundary value
problem is, before using either.

## 2. Hook

Every textbook writes down Ĥψ = Eψ as if it were obvious, then jumps to the answer. The jump hides
the whole idea: an equation is being solved subject to the condition that the electron stays inside
the atom, and the condition — not the equation — is what makes the energies discrete. A guitar
string obeys one wave equation for every pitch; what picks out the notes is that its ends are
clamped. Where this analogy breaks: a string's allowed frequencies are evenly spaced (harmonics),
while a Coulomb well's are not, and the well's spacing depends on n² in the denominator.

## 3. Core idea

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is an eigenvalue problem: the Hamiltonian operator applied
to a wavefunction returns a number times the same wavefunction, and that number is the allowed
energy. Imposing the boundary conditions that ψ is finite, single-valued and vanishes at infinity
admits only a discrete set of solutions. This card derives the box result E_n = n²h²/8mL² in
full. The hydrogen result E_n = −13.6 Z²/n² eV is **quoted**, not derived here: it comes from the
same eigenvalue problem with the Coulomb potential, and its solution needs mathematics beyond
this card. What this card does show is exactly which part of that solution produces which
quantum number, so the quoted formula is not a magic spell.

## 4. Definition

The **Hamiltonian** Ĥ is the operator for total energy: kinetic plus potential. In one dimension
Ĥ = −(ħ²/2m)d²/dx² + V(x). An **eigenfunction** ψ satisfies Ĥψ = Eψ, and E is its **eigenvalue**.
**Normalisation** fixes the constant in ψ so that ∫|ψ|²dV = 1; for the box, A = √(2/L) because
∫₀^L sin²(nπx/L)dx = L/2. A **boundary condition** is a
requirement on ψ at the edges of the region; for a bound state it is ψ → 0 as r → ∞.
**ħ is the reduced Planck constant**, ħ = h/(2π); the 2π is there because one full turn is 2π
radians, so angular frequencies and wave numbers are quoted per radian rather than per cycle.
The Coulomb constant is written k_e = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.988 × 10^9 N m² C^−2 throughout, to keep it
distinct from the wave number.

## 5. How it works

**The calculus used, stated here so no prerequisite is assumed.** The derivative of sin(u) is
cos(u) times du/dx, and the derivative of cos(u) is −sin(u) times du/dx. Applying the rule twice
gives

d/dx sin(κx) = κ cos(κx),   d²/dx² sin(κx) = −κ² sin(κx),

and the same with cos. That single fact is all this card needs. A **boundary value problem** is a
differential equation plus conditions at the edges of the region; the equation has solutions for
many constants, and the edge conditions pick out which ones are physically allowed.

**Step 1: write the equation for the simplest case.** A particle of mass m in a one-dimensional box
of length L has V = 0 inside and infinite walls. The walls are imposed as boundary conditions, not as a term in the potential: V = 0 inside and ψ must
vanish at both walls. Inside,

−(ħ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² = Eψ.

**Step 2: guess the form and let the boundary conditions choose the constants.** For E > 0 the
solutions are sines and cosines: ψ = A sin(κx) + B cos(κx), with κ² = 2mE/ħ². (κ is the wave
number of the state; it is not the Coulomb constant.) Substituting into the equation and using the
derivative above: −(ħ²/2m)(−κ²) sin(κx) = E sin(κx), so κ² = 2mE/ħ², as claimed. The wall at
x = 0 forces ψ(0) = 0, so B = 0. The wall at x = L forces ψ(L) = 0, so sin(κL) = 0 and

κL = nπ, n = 1, 2, 3, …

**Step 3: convert the condition into energies.** With κ = nπ/L and κ² = 2mE/ħ²,

E_n = n²π²ħ²/(2mL²) = n²h²/(8mL²).

The energies are discrete not because energy is intrinsically lumpy but because only certain
wavelengths fit between the walls. Every quantisation result in this unit has the same logical shape:
a boundary condition selects a discrete set of solutions. The mechanism differs from case to case —
walls here, single-valuedness in φ and finiteness in θ for the hydrogen angular part — so do not
assume one derivation transfers.

**Step 4: go to three dimensions, then quote hydrogen honestly.** In 3D the kinetic operator has
three second derivatives, so separating variables produces three integers. In a cubic box they are
independent, and E = (h²/8mL²)(n_x² + n_y² + n_z²).

For the hydrogen atom the potential is V = −Z k_e e²/r, which is spherically symmetric, so
separation gives a radial equation and an angular equation. The two equations do different jobs,
and mixing them up is a standard error:

- The **angular** equations produce the integer m_l, from the requirement that ψ is single-valued
  in φ (a change of 2π must return the same value), and the bound |m_l| ≤ l, from requiring a
  finite solution in θ. They say nothing about n.
- The **radial** equation produces n as one more than the *total* node count: with n_r radial nodes,
  n = n_r + l + 1, and it is the radial equation that imposes l ≤ n − 1. For l ≥ n the series cannot
  terminate, so ψ diverges as r → ∞ and no normalised bound state exists.
- The radial equation also fixes the energies, which come out as E_n = −13.6 Z²/n² eV. This card
  does **not** derive that line — the derivation is a longer exercise in series solutions — but
  it identifies which equation it comes from, and 1.2.1 already showed the empirical ladder it
  reproduces.

**Step 5: what the equation does and does not say.** It says the electron has a wavefunction and
that only certain energies satisfy the boundary conditions. It does not say the electron orbits; the
"orbit" language is a classical import. It also does not include spin: spin is added by hand as a
fourth quantum number m_s, and its existence is evidence that the Schrödinger equation is not the
final theory.

**Step 6: degeneracy as a bookkeeping consequence.** For hydrogen the energy depends only on n, so
all orbitals with the same n are degenerate: n² orbitals, 2n² electrons including spin. Any
additional term in the Hamiltonian that depends on l (repulsion, relativity, external fields) splits
them, which is why the degeneracy is a property of this particular Hamiltonian.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The treatment is non-relativistic and assumes a fixed potential; for multi-electron atoms the
potential includes the other electrons and must be solved self-consistently. It ignores spin, and
therefore cannot explain the fine structure of hydrogen or the periodic table's exact ordering
without extra rules. The particle-in-a-box model treats walls as infinitely high, which is an
idealisation; real confining potentials are finite and the wavefunction leaks out. Finally, ψ is not
directly observable: only |ψ|² and energy differences are.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** An electron in a one-dimensional box of length L = 1.00 nm, with h = 6.626 × 10^−34 J s and
m = 9.109 × 10^−31 kg.

**Hanapin.** E_1 in joules and eV, E_2, and the number of nodes in the n = 3 wavefunction.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: write the formula with units before substituting; the box length must be in
metres.

E_1 = h²/(8mL²) = (6.626 × 10^−34)²/(8 × 9.109 × 10^−31 × (1.00 × 10^−9)²).

Numerator: (6.626 × 10^−34)² = 4.390 × 10^−67. Denominator: 8 × 9.109 × 10^−31 × 10^−18 =
7.287 × 10^−48.
E_1 = 4.390 × 10^−67/7.287 × 10^−48 = 6.024 × 10^−20 J, reported as **6.02 × 10^−20 J**.

In eV: 6.024 × 10^−20/1.602 × 10^−19 = 0.376 eV.

E_2 = 4E_1 = 2.410 × 10^−19 J = 1.50 eV.

Nodes in n = 3: n − 1 = 2 interior nodes, where the sine crosses zero.

**Sagot.** E_1 = 6.02 × 10^−20 J = 0.376 eV; E_2 = 1.50 eV; the n = 3 state has 2 nodes.

## 9. Traps

1. Treating the Schrödinger equation as a force law. It is an energy eigenvalue problem; forces do
   not appear.
2. Thinking quantisation is an extra assumption. It follows from the boundary conditions.
3. Using L as the distance between nodes rather than the box length. The n-th state has n half-waves
   in L.
4. Forgetting the square in n². E_2 is 4E_1, not 2E_1.
5. Confusing ψ with |ψ|². The equation solves for ψ; the measurement predicts |ψ|².
6. Expecting the same level spacing for every potential. Box: gaps grow. Hydrogen: gaps shrink.
7. Writing E_n = n²h²/8mL² for hydrogen. The formula is for a box; hydrogen needs the Coulomb
   potential and gives −13.6/n² eV.

## 10. Check questions

1. Why does the box's ground state have E > 0?
   Because ψ must vanish at both walls, so it must curve; a non-zero second derivative means non-zero
   kinetic energy. E = 0 would need ψ constant, which cannot vanish at the walls.
2. How do the spacings behave as n grows in a box, and in hydrogen?
   Box: E_n ∝ n², so gaps grow. Hydrogen: E_n ∝ −1/n², so gaps shrink toward the ionisation limit.
3. Where do n, l and m_l come from in the hydrogen solution?
   m_l and the bound |m_l| ≤ l come from the angular equations: single-valuedness in φ makes m_l an
   integer, and finiteness in θ bounds it by l. n, the bound l ≤ n − 1 and the energies come from
   the radial equation. A wrong answer often gives the radial results to the angular equation
   because both are "the angular part" in a careless reading; the radial equation is the one whose
   l ≤ n − 1 condition fails at the origin.
4. An electron in a 0.50 nm box: what is E_1 relative to the 1.00 nm case?
   E ∝ 1/L², so halving L quarters L² and makes E_1 four times larger: 2.41 × 10^−19 J = 1.50 eV.
