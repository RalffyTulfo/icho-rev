# Counting of nucleons
## 1. Prerequisites

F.math.1 — unit factors, the mole, powers of ten. You need n = m/M and N = n·N_A in
working order before nucleon counting means anything.

## 2. Hook

A hospital wristband carries three facts you read in three different ways: a name, a date of
birth, and a blood type. A nuclide symbol does the same with one letter and two small integers,
and each of the three says something different about the atom. Read the wrong one as the wrong
kind of fact and everything downstream — molar mass, charge, decay products — comes out wrong.
Where this analogy breaks: a wristband can be misprinted, while a nuclide symbol is a
definition. If the integers contradict the element symbol, the symbol is wrong, not the atom.

## 3. Core idea

Every atom is a nucleus of Z protons and N neutrons, with Z electrons arranged around it when
the atom is neutral. Z fixes which element it is; N fixes which isotope of that element it is.
The two counts are not independent: their sum, the mass number A, is what the top-left
superscript reports, and A − Z = N is where the neutron count comes from.

## 4. Definition

The **atomic number** Z is the number of protons in the nucleus; it is the element's identity.
The **neutron number** N is the count of neutrons. **Nucleons** are protons and neutrons
together, so the **mass number** A = Z + N. A **nuclide** is one specific nucleus, written
^A_Z X, where X is the element symbol, A the superscript at top left and Z the subscript at
bottom left. An **ion** of charge q is written X^q+ or X^q−; the charge counts missing or extra
**electrons**, never nucleons.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: read the symbol position by position.** In ^56_26 Fe the superscript 56 is A and the
subscript 26 is Z. The letter is only a convenience: Z = 26 is what makes it iron, and the
symbol Fe must agree with that number, otherwise the datum is inconsistent. Because Z is
redundant with the symbol, some notation omits it (^56Fe); A is never omitted when the isotope
matters.

**Step 2: get N by subtraction.** A = Z + N rearranges to N = A − Z. For ^56Fe, N = 56 − 26 = 30.
The subtraction works because A counts both kinds of nucleon and Z counts one kind; what is left
must be the other. Adding instead of subtracting gives a plausible-looking number with no
meaning, which is why this is the most common silent error in the topic.

**Step 3: why Z, not A, is the identity.** A neutral atom has exactly Z electrons, because the
nucleus carries charge +Ze and the atom must be neutral. Electrons are what bonds, absorbs light
and reacts, so the chemistry is fixed by Z. Changing Z during a nuclear change produces a
different element; changing N produces the same element with a different mass — an isotope.
That is the whole reason isotopes are chemically almost identical and are separated by physical,
not chemical, methods.

**Step 4: charge is about electrons, not nucleons.** For an ion X^q+, the electron count is
Z − q, because q electrons have been removed from the neutral atom's Z. For ^56Fe^3+, the count
is 26 − 3 = 23 electrons, while Z, N and A stay 26, 30 and 56. Writing Fe^3+ does not mean 3
fewer protons; if it did, the element would have changed.

**Step 5: A is a count, not a mass.** A is an integer number of nucleons; masses are not
integers because a nucleus weighs slightly less than its separated nucleons (the binding energy)
and because each nucleon weighs about 1 u rather than exactly 1 u. The approximation that
matters in calculations is M ≈ A g/mol for a pure nuclide: ^12C is exactly 12 g/mol by
definition, ^56Fe is 55.9349 g/mol, so the approximation is good to about 0.1 % and must be
declared when used. Two refinements belong here. Electrons carry only 1/1836 of a nucleon
mass each, so leaving them out is safe to three figures for light elements. And the deficit
is not sloppiness: it is the nuclear binding energy, about 8.5 MeV per nucleon against a
nucleon rest energy of 931.5 MeV, the same 0.9 % scale that makes fusion exothermic.

**Step 6: counting nucleons in a weighed sample.** Convert mass to moles with n = m/M, then to
particles with N = n·N_A, then to nucleons by multiplying by A: nucleons = (m/M)·N_A·A. Each
factor is a ratio equal to one; the units cancel to a pure count.

**Step 7: the cancellation that is easy to miss.** If M is replaced by its approximation A
 g/mol, the A in the numerator and the A hidden in M cancel, leaving
nucleons ≈ (m / 1 g/mol)·N_A. One gram of any nuclide therefore contains about N_A nucleons,
whatever the element: 1.00 g of ^56Fe holds 1.00/55.9349 × 6.02214 × 10^23 = 1.0767 × 10^22
atoms and 56 times that many nucleons, 6.03 × 10^23. The identity is approximate, not exact,
and the error is the same 0.1 % as the mass approximation.

## 7. Assumptions and limits

The model treats the nucleus as structureless protons and neutrons, which is enough for
counting but fails for binding energy, for the neutron–proton mass difference (~0.14 %), and for
excited nuclear states (isomers, written with an m superscript). M ≈ A g/mol assumes a pure
nuclide; for an element as found in nature it is the weighted average over isotopes and is not
an integer. Nuclear notation also hides the electron configuration, so it cannot tell you the
charge unless the ion charge is written out.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** The nuclide ^56_26 Fe as the ion Fe^3+, and a 1.00 g sample of pure ^12C.

**Hanapin.** For the iron ion: N, the electron count, and the charge in units of e. For the
carbon sample: the number of protons and the total number of nucleons.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: decide which quantities are counts (integers) and which are masses, and
write the nuclide symbol before touching numbers. Iron: Z = 26 from the symbol, A = 56 from the
superscript, so N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30. A 3+ ion has lost three electrons, so electrons
= 26 − 3 = 23, and the charge is +3e. Nucleons stay at 56, because neither protons nor neutrons
left the nucleus.

Carbon: M(^12C) = 12.000 g/mol exactly, so n = 1.00 g / 12.000 g/mol = 8.3333 × 10^−2 mol,
atoms = 8.3333 × 10^−2 × 6.02214 × 10^23 = 5.0184 × 10^22, protons = 6 × 5.0184 × 10^22 =
3.0111 × 10^23, nucleons = 12 × 5.0184 × 10^22 = 6.0221 × 10^23. The last number equals N_A
because one mole of ^12C weighs exactly 12 g and each atom contributes 12 nucleons.

**Sagot.** Fe^3+: N = 30, 23 electrons, charge +3e. 1.00 g ^12C: 3.01 × 10^23 protons and
6.02 × 10^23 nucleons.

## 9. Traps

1. Using A as a molar mass without saying so. For ^56Fe, 56 g/mol is an approximation; the
   nuclide's actual molar mass is 55.9349 g/mol, and the element's is 55.845 g/mol.
2. Counting A as the number of protons in an ion of charge q, then subtracting q from it. The
   charge removes electrons only, so the proton count is Z whatever the charge is.
3. Computing N = A + Z. The only sensible reading is N = A − Z, and N = 0 for ^1H is the check
   that confirms it.
4. Writing a nuclide's charge from its group. Fe^3+ is a chemistry statement about the ion, not
   about the nucleus; the nucleus is still Z = 26.
5. Forgetting that a change in Z changes the element. A decay that removes two protons does not
   give an isotope of the parent; it gives a different element.

## 10. Check questions

1. For ^40_20 Ca^2+, give N and the electron count.
   Answer: N = 40 − 20 = 20, electrons = 20 − 2 = 18. Two wrong answers tempt: reporting 18
   for N as well (subtracting the charge twice), and computing N = 40 − 20 − 2, which treats
   the lost electrons as lost nucleons.
2. Which pair are isotopes: ^14N and ^15N, or ^14N and ^14C?
   ^14N and ^15N, same Z = 7. The second pair has different Z, so they are different elements;
   the shared mass number is a coincidence that the notation makes look meaningful.
3. How many nucleons are in 0.500 mol of ^235U?
   Answer: 0.500 × 6.02214 × 10^23 × 235 = 7.08 × 10^25. The tempting answer 0.500 × 235 = 118
   counts moles of nucleons, not nucleons.
4. Why is the molar mass of iron 55.845 g/mol rather than exactly 56?
   Because natural iron is a mixture of isotopes with masses near 54, 56, 57 and 58, and because
   even ^56Fe weighs 55.9349 u, not 56 u, once binding energy is included.
