# Nuclear reactions
## 1. Prerequisites

1.3.1 for decay bookkeeping and 1.1.1 for A and Z. This card extends the same conservation laws to
reactions that are induced rather than spontaneous, and adds the energy calculation.

## 2. Hook

Rutherford fired alpha particles at nitrogen and got oxygen and a proton out. Nobody had done that
before, and the equation that describes it looks exactly like the decay equations of the previous
card: A and Z balance, and the rest is bookkeeping. The new ingredient is energy: a reaction runs by
itself only if the products weigh less than the reactants, and the missing mass appears as kinetic
energy. Where this analogy breaks: a chemical equation balances atoms exactly, while a nuclear
equation can convert mass into energy, so the mass balance is only approximate and the deficit is the
point.

## 3. Core idea

A nuclear reaction is balanced when A and Z each match on both sides. Whether it releases energy is a
separate question answered by the mass defect: Q = Δm c², with 1 u ≡ 931.494 MeV, and Q > 0 for a
spontaneous reaction. Alpha and beta decays are one class of nuclear reaction; bombardment,
fission and fusion are others, and all obey the same conservation laws.

## 4. Definition

A **nuclear reaction** is a rearrangement of nucleons, written with A and Z conserved. The **Q
**Q value** is the energy released, Q = (mass of reactants − mass of products)c²; positive Q means
exothermic. Some tables define Q with the opposite sign, so state the convention before quoting a
number.
free nucleons. **Binding energy per nucleon** is the mass defect of a nucleus divided by A, converted
to energy.

## 5. How it works

**Step 1: balance A and Z.** Write every species with its A as superscript and Z as subscript,
including the projectile and the emitted particle. Solve A first, then Z, because only some particles
carry A.

**Step 2: the projectile changes the accounting.** For bombardment, add the projectile's A and Z to
the target's. Example: ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H. A: 14 + 4 = 18 and 17 + 1 = 18. Z: 7 + 2 = 9 and
8 + 1 = 9.

**Step 3: chains are solved in a fixed order.** In a decay chain ending at a stable nuclide, the
number of alphas is fixed by the A difference: n_α = (A_parent − A_daughter)/4. Then Z fixes the
number of beta-minus decays: n_β = Z_daughter − (Z_parent − 2n_α). For ²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb: n_α = 32/4 = 8,
n_β = 82 − (92 − 16) = 6. Check both totals before moving on.

**Step 4: Q from masses.** Q = (Σm_reactants − Σm_products)c². With masses in u, multiply the mass
difference by 931.494 MeV/u. For ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + α:

Δm = 238.050788 − 234.043601 − 4.002603 = 0.004584 u,
Q = 0.004584 × 931.494 = 4.270 MeV.

**Step 5: where the energy goes.** Momentum conservation forces the products to share Q inversely as
their masses, so the light alpha carries almost all of it: 234/238 × 4.270 = 4.198 MeV. That is the
sharp alpha line seen in a detector.

**Step 6: why fusion and fission both work.** Binding energy per nucleon rises from hydrogen to a
peak near ⁵⁶Fe at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, then falls slowly to about 7.6 MeV for ²³⁸U. Any process
that moves nucleons up this curve releases energy: fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy ones
both do, and iron-56 is the end point for both.

**Step 7: fission and fusion still balance.** ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n: A: 236 = 141 + 92 + 3;
Z: 92 = 56 + 36. The three neutrons are what make a chain reaction possible: if at least one is captured by another
²³⁵U nucleus, the process repeats. The Sun's net fusion,
4¹H → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν, balances A (4 = 4) and Z (4 = 2 + 2).

## 7. Assumptions and limits

Atomic masses can be used for beta decay: the difference in electron counts between parent and daughter
already accounts for the emitted or absorbed electron, with the neutrino mass neglected. Nuclear masses
are needed only when the electron is not part of the tabulated atom, as in a bare-nucleus reaction.
The binding-energy curve is an average trend, not a prediction of every reaction's Q. The treatment
ignores angular momentum barriers, which is why fusion needs high temperature even though it is
energetically favourable, and ignores the branching of fission into many product pairs.

## 8. Worked example

**Given.** ²³²Th decays to ²⁰⁸Pb by a chain of alphas and beta-minuses. Masses: ²³⁸U 238.050788 u,
²³⁴Th 234.043601 u, ⁴He 4.002603 u, 1 u = 931.494 MeV.

**Hanapin.** The alpha and beta counts for the thorium chain, and Q for the uranium alpha decay.

**Solusyon.** Step 0: for the chain, do A first; a beta changes nothing there.

n_α = (232 − 208)/4 = 24/4 = 6.
After six alphas, Z = 90 − 12 = 78; lead is 82, so n_β = 82 − 78 = 4.
Check: A: −24 + 0 = −24 → 208. Z: −12 + 4 = −8 → 82.

For Q: Δm = 238.050788 − 234.043601 − 4.002603 = 0.004584 u.
Q = 0.004584 × 931.494 = 4.270 MeV; the alpha carries 234/238 of it, 4.198 MeV.

**Sagot.** Six alphas and four beta-minuses; Q = 4.270 MeV for the uranium decay.

## 9. Traps

1. Counting betas from A. Betas change Z only; alphas change both.
2. Getting the beta count sign wrong: the chain's Z must rise from 76 to 82 in the uranium case, so
   betas are beta-minus, not beta-plus.
3. Forgetting the projectile's A and Z in a bombardment reaction.
4. Using atomic masses for beta decay without accounting for the electron.
5. Reporting Q as negative for a spontaneous decay. Mass lost means Q > 0.
6. Assuming a balanced equation is spontaneous. Balance is necessary, not sufficient.
7. Claiming fission releases energy because "heavy nuclei are unstable". The reason is the position
   on the binding-energy curve, not instability in general.

## 10. Check questions

1. ²³⁵U absorbs a neutron and fissions to ¹⁴¹Ba and ⁹²Kr. How many neutrons are released?
   A: 235 + 1 = 236; products 141 + 92 = 233; so 3 neutrons.
2. Why does fusion of hydrogen release energy if the product is lighter than the reactants?
   The helium-4 nucleus has a larger binding energy per nucleon, so its mass is lower by Δm, and
   Δm c² appears as kinetic energy.
3. In the ²³⁸U chain, why are there 8 alphas and 6 betas rather than 6 alphas and 8 betas?
   A fixes the alpha count (32/4 = 8); the remaining Z deficit of 6 must come from beta-minus decays.
4. Why is iron-56 the endpoint of both fusion and fission energy release?
   It sits at the maximum of binding energy per nucleon, so moving either direction away from it
   costs energy rather than releasing it.
