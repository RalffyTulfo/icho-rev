# General Chemical Kinetics

Lowkey, this is where chemistry goes from static to dynamic. We're not just looking at what happens—we're watching how fast it happens. Imagine a reaction like a race: some reactions are lightning fast, others take forever. Kinetics tells us why. Para mas madali na buhay natin, let's break down how reactions proceed and what controls their speed.

---

## 🕯🔥 Terms & Definitions 🔥🕯

> **ICHO Grading Tip:** Always specify units for rate constants and concentrations. Never leave out the units for time (usually seconds or minutes). Magagalit graders sayo pag linaktawan mo 'to.

1. **Rate of Reaction**: The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. For a general reaction $aA + bB 
ightarrow cC + dD$, the rate is defined as:
  $$	ext{Rate} = -rac{1}{a}rac{\Delta[A]}{\Delta t} = -rac{1}{b}rac{\Delta[B]}{\Delta t} = rac{1}{c}rac{\Delta[C]}{\Delta t} = rac{1}{d}rac{\Delta[D]}{\Delta t}$$ 

1. **Rate Law**: An equation that relates the rate of a reaction to the concentrations of reactants. It is always determined experimentally and cannot be predicted from the stoichiometry alone.
  $$	ext{Rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n$$
  where $k$ is the rate constant, and $m$ and $n$ are the reaction orders with respect to $A$ and $B$, respectively.

1. **Reaction Order**: The exponent in the rate law to which the concentration of a reactant is raised. Zero order means the rate is independent of that reactant's concentration.

1. **Rate Constant ($k$)**: A proportionality constant that relates the rate of the reaction to the concentrations of reactants. It is temperature-dependent and specific to each reaction.

1. **Half-Life ($t_{1/2}$)**: The time required for the concentration of a reactant to decrease to half of its initial value. For a first-order reaction, $t_{1/2} = rac{\ln(2)}{k}$.

1. **Pseudo-First-Order Reaction**: A reaction that appears first-order due to one reactant being in large excess, effectively making its concentration constant.

1. **Transition State Theory**: A theory that explains reaction rates in terms of the formation of an activated complex (transition state) that must be overcome for the reaction to proceed.

1. **Arrhenius Equation**: An equation that relates the rate constant to temperature and activation energy:
  $$k = A e^{-E_a/(RT)}$$ 
  where $A$ is the pre-exponential factor, $E_a$ is the activation energy, $R$ is the gas constant, and $T$ is the temperature in Kelvin.

## 💥 Why Does This Exist? (Physical Meaning) 💥

Thermodynamics tells us if a reaction is possible, but kinetics tells us how fast it happens. Imagine a reaction with a negative Gibbs free energy change—it's thermodynamically favorable, but if it's too slow, it might as well not happen in practice. Kinetics is crucial for:

1. **Industrial Processes**: How fast can we produce ammonia in the Haber process?
2. **Drug Design**: How quickly does a drug bind to its target?
3. **Environmental Chemistry**: How fast do pollutants degrade in the atmosphere?
4. **Food Chemistry**: How long does it take for milk to spoil?

In IChO, kinetics problems often involve determining rate laws, calculating half-lives, or analyzing temperature effects on reaction rates. You'll need to be comfortable with both experimental data interpretation and theoretical models.

## 3. Derivation from First Principles

### 3.1 Rate Laws and Reaction Orders

The rate law for a reaction is always determined experimentally. For a general reaction:

$$aA + bB 
ightarrow cC + dD$$

The rate law is:

$$	ext{Rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n$$

where $m$ and $n$ are the **experimental** orders, which may or may not match the stoichiometric coefficients $a$ and $b$.

**Example**: For the reaction $2NO(g) + O_2(g) 
ightarrow 2NO_2(g)$, the rate law might be:

$$	ext{Rate} = k[NO]^2[O_2]$$

Here, the order with respect to $NO$ is 2, and the order with respect to $O_2$ is 1, even though the stoichiometric coefficients are both 1.

### 3.2 Determining Reaction Order from Experimental Data

To determine the order of a reaction, we use the method of initial rates. Suppose we have the following data for the reaction $A 
ightarrow B$:

| Experiment | [A] (M) | Initial Rate (M/s) |
|------------|--------|---------------------|
| 1          | 0.1    | 0.015              |
| 2          | 0.2    | 0.060              |
| 3          | 0.3    | 0.135             |

To find the order with respect to $A$, we compare the change in rate with the change in concentration:

$$rac{	ext{Rate}_2}{	ext{Rate}_1} = rac{0.060}{0.015} = 4 = rac{[A]_2}{[A]_1}^m = rac{0.2}{0.1}^m = 2^m$$

Solving for $m$: $4 = 2^m 
ightarrow m = 2$. So, the reaction is second order with respect to $A$.

For different reaction orders, the integrated rate laws and half-lives are different:

1. **Zero-Order Reaction**:

### 3.4 Temperature Dependence: Arrhenius Equation

The Arrhenius equation relates the rate constant $k$ to temperature $T$ and activation energy $E_a$:

$$k = A e^{-E_a/(RT)}$$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$	ext{ln}(k) = 	ext{ln}(A) - rac{E_a}{R} rac{1}{T}$$

This equation shows that a plot of $	ext{ln}(k)$ vs. $1/T$ should be linear with a slope of $-E_a/R$.

## 💔 Common IChO Traps 💔

1. **Assuming Reaction Order from Stoichiometry**: The order of a reaction is determined experimentally and may not match the stoichiometric coefficients.

2. **Incorrect Half-Life Calculations**: Remember that the half-life for a first-order reaction is constant, but for zero-order and second-order reactions, it depends on the initial concentration.

3. **Sign Errors in Arrhenius Equation**: Always double-check the signs when rearranging the Arrhenius equation. A negative sign error can lead to incorrect activation energy values.

4. **Units**: Ensure that all units are consistent. For example, if concentration is in mol/L, the rate constant units should reflect that.

5. **Temperature Conversions**: Always convert temperatures to Kelvin when using the Arrhenius equation.

## Recall Cards

1. **Recall Card 1**: What is the difference between a reaction being first-order and second-order?
  Answer: A first-order reaction's rate depends linearly on the concentration of one reactant, while a second-order reaction's rate depends on the square of the concentration of one reactant or the product of the concentrations of two reactants.

1. **Recall Card 2**: How does increasing temperature affect the rate constant $k$?
  Answer: Increasing temperature increases the rate constant $k$ because it increases the fraction of molecules with energy greater than the activation energy, as described by the Arrhenius equation.

1. **Recall Card 3**: What is the half-life of a first-order reaction?
  Answer: The half-life of a first-order reaction is constant and given by $t_{1/2} = rac{	ext{ln}(2)}{k}$.
