# Inorganic Chemistry: Descriptive Chemistry and Periodic Properties

## Terms & Definitions

- **Inert Pair Effect**: Relativistic stabilization of s-valence electrons in heavy post-transition p-block elements (e.g., Tl+, Pb2+, Bi3+), making lower oxidation states more stable.
- **Oxoacid Strength**: Acid strength increases with the oxidation number of the central atom and the number of terminal oxo (=O) groups (Pauling's rules).
- **Zintl Phase**: Stoichiometric intermetallic compounds formed between alkali/alkaline-earth metals and post-transition p-block elements, featuring polyanionic clusters.
- **Latimer Diagram**: Summary diagram showing standard reduction potentials for various oxidation states of an element in acidic or basic solution.
- **Frost Diagram**: Graph of nE standard (or Delta G standard / F) versus oxidation state, used to identify disproportionation and stability of species.

## Why Does This Exist?

Descriptive inorganic chemistry covers the vast periodic table behavior of main-group elements, transition metals, acids, bases, and redox reactions. IChO exams frequently test synthesis, reactivity, and thermodynamic stability using Frost and Latimer diagrams.

## Derivation from First Principles

### Frost Diagrams and Disproportionation
In a Frost diagram, the vertical axis plots nE standard (proportional to -Delta G standard / F), and the horizontal axis plots oxidation state (N).
- **Thermodynamic Stability**: Species located at the bottom of a Frost diagram are thermodynamically stable against reduction/oxidation.
- **Disproportionation**: A species is unstable toward disproportionation if it lies above the line connecting its adjacent neighbors (concave upward curvature: E standard right > E standard left).
- **Comproportionation**: Species lying below the line connecting neighbors will spontaneously undergo comproportionation to form the intermediate oxidation state.

## Worked Example

## Step 0: Interpret Frost Diagram Coordinates (nE standard vs Oxidation State)
**Problem**: Given a Frost diagram for manganese in acidic solution where Mn3+ lies above the line connecting Mn2+ and MnO2, determine whether Mn3+ is stable against disproportionation.

**Solution**:
1. **Condition for Disproportionation**: A species X disproportionates into X+ and X- if its point on a Frost diagram lies concave up (higher in free energy) than the straight line connecting the adjacent oxidation states.
2. **Analysis**: Mn3+ (N=+3) lies vertically above the tie-line connecting Mn2+ (N=+2) and Mn4+ (MnO2, N=+4).
3. **Reaction**:
   2Mn3+ + 2H2O -> Mn2+ + MnO2 + 4H+
4. **Conclusion**: Mn3+ is thermodynamically unstable and spontaneously disproportionates in water.

**Sanity Check**: Concave upward curvature on a Frost diagram always indicates thermodynamic instability toward disproportionation into adjacent states.

## Common IChO Traps

1. **Latimer to Frost Conversion**: When calculating Delta G standard across multiple steps in a Latimer diagram, remember that free energies (Delta G standard = -nFE standard) are additive, whereas standard reduction potentials (E standard) are NOT additive.
2. **Acid vs Base Medium**: Always verify whether potentials are given in acidic (H+) or basic (OH-) solution, as E standard values shift dramatically with pH.

## Recall Cards
- **Recall Card 1**: What feature on a Frost diagram indicates that a species will undergo disproportionation?
  Answer: A concave-up position (lying above the straight line connecting its adjacent oxidation states).
- **Recall Card 2**: Are standard reduction potentials (E standard) additive when combining half-reactions?
  Answer: No! You must convert E standard values to free energy changes (Delta G standard = -nFE standard), sum them, and convert back.
