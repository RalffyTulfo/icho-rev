# Thermodynamics: Calorimetry and Heat Capacity

## Terms & Definitions

- **Calorimetry**: The science of measuring heat transfer.
- **Constant-Volume Calorimetry**: A method of measuring heat transfer in a system where the volume is kept constant.
- **Constant-Pressure Calorimetry**: A method of measuring heat transfer in a system where the pressure is kept constant.
- **Heat Capacity ($C$)**: The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree. $C_p$ and $C_v$ are heat capacities at constant pressure and constant volume, respectively.
- **Kirchhoff's Law of Thermochemistry**: The enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the path taken, and the enthalpy change for a reaction at a given temperature is equal to the sum of the enthalpy changes for the individual steps in the path.


## Why Does This Exist?

Calorimetry is essential for measuring heat transfer in chemical and physical processes. Understanding heat capacity and Kirchhoff's Law allows us to analyze and predict the behavior of systems under different conditions. This lesson introduces the fundamental concepts of calorimetry, heat capacity, and Kirchhoff's Law, and explains how these concepts are applied in practical scenarios.


## Derivation from First Principles

### Constant-Volume and Constant-Pressure Calorimetry

- **Constant-Volume Calorimetry**: In a constant-volume calorimeter, the volume of the system is kept constant. The heat added to the system is used to increase the internal energy of the system. The change in internal energy is given by:

$$[3m\Delta U = q_v[0m$$

where $q_v$ is the heat added at constant volume.

- **Constant-Pressure Calorimetry**: In a constant-pressure calorimeter, the pressure of the system is kept constant. The heat added to the system is used to increase the enthalpy of the system. The change in enthalpy is given by:

$$[3m\Delta H = q_p[0m$$

where $q_p$ is the heat added at constant pressure.

### Heat Capacity

- **$C_p$**: Heat capacity at constant pressure. It represents the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree at constant pressure.
- **$C_v$**: Heat capacity at constant volume. It represents the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree at constant volume.

For an ideal gas, the relationship between $C_p$ and $C_v$ is given by Mayer's relation:

$$[3mC_p - C_v = R[0m$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314 \, \text{J} \cdot \text{mol}^{-1} \cdot \text{K}^{-1}$).

### Kirchhoff's Law of Thermochemistry

Kirchhoff's Law states that the enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the path taken, and the enthalpy change for a reaction at a given temperature is equal to the sum of the enthalpy changes for the individual steps in the path. This law is expressed as:

$$[3m\Delta H(T_2) = \Delta H(T_1) + \int_{T_1}^{T_2} \Delta C_p \, dT[0m$$

where $\Delta H(T_1)$ and $\Delta H(T_2)$ are the enthalpy changes at temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$, respectively, and $\Delta C_p$ is the change in heat capacity at constant pressure.


## Worked Example

**Problem**: Calculate the change in enthalpy ($[3m\Delta H[0m$) for a reaction where 50 J of heat is added to a system at constant pressure, and the system does 20 J of work on its surroundings.

**Solution**:

Using the First Law of Thermodynamics:

$$[3m\Delta U = q + w[0m$$

Substitute the given values:

$$[3m\Delta U = 50 \, \text{J} + (-20 \, \text{J}) = 30 \, \text{J}[0m$$

For a constant-pressure process, the change in enthalpy is equal to the heat added:

$$[3m\Delta H = q_p = 50 \, \text{J}[0m$$

**Sanity Check**: The enthalpy change is 50 J, which makes sense because the heat added to the system is 50 J, and the work done by the system is 20 J, resulting in a net increase in enthalpy.


## Common IChO Traps

1. **Sign Conventions**: Ensure you are consistent with the sign conventions for heat and work. In many contexts, work done by the system is negative, while work done on the system is positive.

2. **Path vs. State Functions**: Do not confuse state functions with path functions. For example, internal energy is a state function, while heat and work are path functions.

3. **Units**: Always ensure that units are consistent and correctly applied. For example, $R$ is in J/mol·K, so ensure that your calculations align with these units.
