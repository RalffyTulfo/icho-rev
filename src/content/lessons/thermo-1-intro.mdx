# Thermodynamics: Energy and the First Law

## Terms & Definitions

- **Energy**: The capacity to do work or transfer heat.
- **First Law of Thermodynamics**: Energy is conserved; the change in internal energy of a system is equal to the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system: $\Delta U = q + w$.
- **Heat ($q$)**: Energy transferred due to a temperature difference.
- **Work ($w$)**: Energy transferred by a force acting through a distance (e.g., expansion/compression of gases).
- **Internal Energy ($[3m\Delta U[0m$)**: The total energy contained within a system, including kinetic and potential energy of molecules.
- **State Function**: A property that depends only on the current state of the system, not on the path taken to reach that state (e.g., internal energy, enthalpy).
- **Path Function**: A property that depends on the path taken to reach a state (e.g., work, heat).
- **Enthalpy ($[3m\Delta H[0m$)**: Defined as $\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V$, where $P$ is pressure and $\Delta V$ is volume change.
- **Heat Capacity ($C$)**: The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree. $C_p$ and $C_v$ are heat capacities at constant pressure and constant volume, respectively.
- **Mayer's Relation**: For an ideal gas, $C_p - C_v = R$, where $R$ is the universal gas constant.


## Why Does This Exist?

Thermodynamics is the foundation of understanding energy transformations in chemical and physical processes. The First Law establishes the principle of energy conservation, which is critical for analyzing systems in chemistry, physics, and engineering. This lesson introduces the fundamental concepts of energy, heat, work, and internal energy, and explains how these quantities relate to each other through the First Law.


## Derivation from First Principles

### Energy and the First Law

The First Law of Thermodynamics is derived from the principle of energy conservation. It states that the change in internal energy ($[3m\Delta U[0m$) of a system is equal to the heat added to the system ($q$) minus the work done by the system ($w$):

$$[3m\Delta U = q + w[0m$$

- **Heat ($q$)**: When heat is added to a system, its internal energy increases. Conversely, if heat is removed, the internal energy decreases.
- **Work ($w$)**: Work is done when a system expands or contracts against an external pressure. For a reversible process, work is given by $w = -\int P \, dV$.

### State Functions vs. Path Functions

- **State Functions**: Properties like internal energy ($[3m\Delta U[0m$) and enthalpy ($[3m\Delta H[0m$) depend only on the initial and final states of the system, not on the path taken.
- **Path Functions**: Properties like heat ($q$) and work ($w$) depend on the specific path taken to reach the final state.

### Enthalpy

Enthalpy ($[3m\Delta H[0m$) is defined as:

$$[3m\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V[0m$$

Enthalpy is particularly useful for processes occurring at constant pressure, such as reactions in open containers.

### Heat Capacities

- **$C_p$**: Heat capacity at constant pressure. It represents the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree at constant pressure.
- **$C_v$**: Heat capacity at constant volume. It represents the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree at constant volume.

For an ideal gas, the relationship between $C_p$ and $C_v$ is given by Mayer's relation:

$$[3mC_p - C_v = R[0m$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314 \, \text{J} \cdot \text{mol}^{-1} \cdot \text{K}^{-1}$).


## Worked Example

**Problem**: Calculate the change in internal energy ($[3m\Delta U[0m$) for a system where 100 J of heat is added to it, and the system does 40 J of work on its surroundings.

**Solution**:

Using the First Law of Thermodynamics:

$$[3m\Delta U = q + w[0m$$

Substitute the given values:

$$[3m\Delta U = 100 \, \text{J} + (-40 \, \text{J}) = 60 \, \text{J}[0m$$

**Sanity Check**: The internal energy increases by 60 J, which makes sense because more heat was added to the system than the work done by the system.


## Common IChO Traps

1. **Sign Conventions**: Ensure you are consistent with the sign conventions for heat and work. In many contexts, work done by the system is negative, while work done on the system is positive.

2. **Path vs. State Functions**: Do not confuse state functions with path functions. For example, internal energy is a state function, while heat and work are path functions.

3. **Units**: Always ensure that units are consistent and correctly applied. For example, $R$ is in J/mol·K, so ensure that your calculations align with these units.
